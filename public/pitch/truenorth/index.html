Section one

What we found.

First, what this is not. It is not a critique of your ice cream or your shops. You make it in the shop, daily, and Marshall alone has 625 Google reviews averaging 4.7. The product is not the problem. The problem is that the website undersells it, and the case you rebuild behind the counter every day never reaches anybody who is not standing in front of it.

Worth saying too, because it is true: plenty on your site is right. Both shop numbers in your header are tap-to-call, so a customer on a phone reaches you in one press. Your flavors page carries allergen marks. Your menu publishes real prices instead of asking people to phone for them. Your copyright line reads 2026. Every photograph on your homepage is served from your own address, exactly one script on the page comes from anybody else's server, and there is no web company's name in your footer using your shop as an advertisement. Plenty of local businesses can say none of that. What follows is the part that is costing you customers, read out of your own pages on September 4, 2026, with a link on every claim so you can check it yourself.

The hours contradict themselves on the same screen

The strip across the top of every page reads Mon-Sun 12-9pm. The first paragraph of your homepage, sitting directly underneath it, reads Open 2-9pm every day. Both numbers are correct. Marshall opens at noon and Battle Creek opens at two. Nothing on the page says which is which, so a customer reads one screen that disagrees with itself and believes whichever line their eye landed on.

Search engines have no business record for you

Underneath every page sits a block of machine-readable facts, the part a search engine reads instead of guessing. Yours describes a website. It carries a breadcrumb trail, an organization, a page, a site and a search box. It carries no street address, no opening time, no phone number and no menu, for either shop. Your addresses, your hours and your entire price list are on the site for people and exist nowhere a machine can read them, which is why Google is left inventing your hours out of that one sentence.

Section two

The part that matters most.

There is no single place that knows you have two shops

Your site holds one hours value, one description, one map and one flavor list, because it was built when there was one shop. When Battle Creek opened, nothing underneath it had a slot to put a second shop in. Somebody typed the second phone number into the header strip, which is the only place it would fit, and everything else stayed pointed at Marshall.

That is the whole of section one. It is why the hours argue with themselves, why the description carries the wrong store's opening time, why the map knows one street, why the flavors page cannot tell a customer which case a flavor is in, and why there is no page for a Battle Creek search to land on. It is what a one-shop website looks like two shops later.

Every surface on the rebuild resolves a shop before it answers a customer's question. Not a location toggle bolted onto a single-shop site. Two shops, all the way down, so a third one is an entry in a file rather than a rebuild.

You own it. All of it.

Written down, not implied. When the build fee is paid in full, these are yours: the design, the source code, the content, the accounts, the code repository, the hosting project, and every login to them. The domain stays registered in your name and transfers to any registrar you name, free, whenever you ask. After launch it is month to month, thirty days' notice, no penalty, and the site keeps working on the way out the door. The terms are one plain-English page published at glazedweb.com/agreement, the same page every client gets.

Nothing underneath the site is rented. No page-builder license, no form service, no booking platform taking a cut, no plugin bill arriving in a year. If a feature can be written instead of rented, we write it.

Three questions worth asking any web vendor, including us

1. If we stop paying you, what do we keep? 2. Whose name is the domain registered in? 3. Can we leave in thirty days? Our answers are everything, yours, and yes. Ask us the same three in person and hold us to them.

One thing this letter is not going to pretend. Your current setup is not a hostage situation. Your site sits on hosting you pay for, the photographs are served from your own address, and no web company's name is in your footer using your shop as its portfolio. A great many small businesses cannot say that, and you can. The problem with your website is not who holds it. It is that it was built to describe one shop and is now being asked to run two.

Section four

The board is already live.

Your own flavors page says it better than we could: Our Flavors are Always Changing! The trouble is that keeping a website current is somebody's job, and at nine o'clock at night in a shop it is nobody's job. So we did not build a way for us to update your flavors. We wired the website to the case.

How it works

You tap a tub out. The website follows.

You tap. Sign in at scooplist.glazedweb.com/login/truenorth on the phone in your apron. Tap the flavor, tap Tub's empty, take it off the board. Two taps and it is off the site. Putting the next one in is the same two taps the other way, from a library of every flavor you have ever churned.

The site follows. Every board on the website reads the same feed on a sixty second cycle. No deploy, nobody to text, nothing to remember, and no second list to keep in step with the first.

If it breaks, nobody sees it. The site gives the feed three seconds to answer. On any failure every board falls back to the last published list and the page renders exactly as it did before any of this existed. A menu that sometimes fails is worse than one that is occasionally a day stale, so the feed is an enhancement and never a dependency.

Three ways to check that, none of them a screenshot. The first is the machine's own answer: ask the site what it is reading and it replies "source":"scooplist", "reachable":true, "status":200. The second is the board rendering from that feed. The third is the same case as a wall board, sized for a screen behind the counter, which comes with it.

Section five

The part that can take money.

Catering is a big ticket that arrives as one phone call

Your published price list runs to a tier called 301 and up at $3.75 a head for homemade. One party at that size is a bigger ticket than a good Saturday, and the way to start one today is a Gmail address and a phone number that cannot be tapped.

What belongs there is a qualifying form rather than a contact form: the date, the headcount, which shop, whether they want homemade, soft serve or sandwiches, and room for the rest. It arrives as a brief you can price without a phone call, from the person who was going to think about calling you and then did not. That form is already on the demo. What it asks is the part worth arguing about in person, because you know what makes an inquiry useful the moment it lands and we do not.

Pre-orders, built and switched off

The order page and the code behind it work end to end today. Item names and prices resolve on the server from your own menu, so a tampered order cannot invent a price. Quantities clamp. An item the chosen shop does not carry is refused rather than accepted and disappointed. It works with JavaScript switched off.

And it is off, behind a single switch, because an order button put in front of an owner promises a service nobody has turned on. It takes no cards. Version one is order ahead and pay at the counter, exactly as your register already works. Taking cards online needs a payment account in your name, is a separate decision and a separate quote, and this letter is not going to imply otherwise.

We have no idea how much of your revenue catering is today, and nothing in this letter assumes an answer. It is the first question we would ask you, because if it is a bigger number than it looks from outside then the site should be built around it rather than beside it.
